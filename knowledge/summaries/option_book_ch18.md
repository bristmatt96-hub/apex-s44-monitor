# Chapter 18: Models and the Real World

## Key Concepts
- **Model assumptions vs. reality**: Traditional pricing models (Black-Scholes, Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) rest on six key assumptions, all of which are violated in practice: (1) frictionless markets, (2) constant interest rates, (3) constant volatility, (4) continuous trading/no price gaps, (5) volatility independent of price direction, (6) lognormal distribution of prices
- **Markets are not frictionless**: Transaction costs, margin requirements, short-sale restrictions, and borrowing/lending rate differences all affect real-world pricing. Transaction costs are the most serious flaw — especially for high-gamma strategies requiring frequent rehedging
- **Volatility is NOT constant**: Real-world volatility clusters (periods of high vol followed by more high vol). The order in which volatility occurs matters — high vol near expiration has far more impact on ATM options (high gamma) than high vol early in the option's life
- **Price gaps (jump risk)**: Markets do not follow a pure diffusion process — they gap, especially overnight and around news events. Gaps devastate short gamma positions near expiration. With 9 months to expiry, a 5-point gap is manageable; with 1 day, it can be catastrophic
- **Expiration straddles**: Because models undervalue ATM options near expiration (they can't price jump risk), experienced traders often BUY ATM straddles near expiry rather than selling them. The strategy loses more often than it wins, but the occasional gap makes it profitable long-term
- **Volatility depends on price direction**: Stock indexes tend to be more volatile when falling (leverage effect), while commodities tend to be more volatile when rising. The CEV (Constant Elasticity of Variance) model captures this but is rarely used in practice
- **Fat tails (leptokurtosis)**: Real-world price distributions have more small moves, more very large moves, and fewer medium moves than a normal distribution predicts. The kurtosis of virtually all markets is positive
- **Volatility skew**: Implied volatility varies by strike price. The shape of the skew reflects the market's view of the model's inadequacies — higher IV for OTM options compensates for the model's underestimate of tail risk. The skew can be expressed as a function of ln(E/U)/√t to normalize across time and price

## Trading Rules & Practical Takeaways
- **Never sell large quantities of ATM options near expiration**: This is the single most dangerous position in options trading. A price gap can cause unlimited losses that no model predicted. Risk managers should flag this as a hard limit
- **Consider buying ATM straddles near expiration**: When ATM options are priced below theoretical value (sellers overshoot on time decay), buying them exploits the model's inability to price jump risk. Accept that most will expire worthless — the occasional gap pays for all the losses
- **Transaction costs erode theoretical edge**: High-gamma strategies requiring frequent delta adjustments are especially vulnerable. Factor in realistic transaction costs before committing to any vol-trading strategy
- **Use the volatility skew as a sixth input**: Treat the skew as an additional variable in the pricing model. Shift the entire skew up/down based on your vol view, and left/right as the underlying moves. The function f(x) where x = ln(E/U)/√t can parameterize the skew
- **Model-generated values become unreliable near expiration**: Experienced traders rely less on models and more on experience/intuition as expiration approaches. The diffusion assumption breaks down most severely for short-dated ATM options
- **Skewness tells you about directional vol bias**: Negative skewness (stock indexes) means the market expects larger moves downward. Positive skewness (commodities) means larger upward moves expected. This informs directional strategy selection

## Application to Credit-Equity Bridge Strategy
- **Fat tails are our friend**: Our entire strategy is a bet on fat-tail events — credit deterioration that leads to equity price gaps. The leptokurtic nature of real distributions means these events happen more often than models predict, making our OTM puts more valuable than Black-Scholes suggests
- **Volatility skew impacts our strike selection**: OTM puts have higher IV than ATM puts (the skew). When we buy 10% OTM puts, we're paying a premium for the skew. However, the skew exists BECAUSE these events happen — it's fair compensation for real tail risk. Our credit signals give us an informational edge on WHETHER the tail event will materialise
- **Gap risk IS our trade thesis**: We are betting that a credit signal (CDS widening, filing alert, maturity wall) precedes an equity price gap. Chapter 18 confirms that gaps happen, models can't price them, and the market partially compensates via the skew — but our credit signals give us superior timing
- **Stochastic volatility validates our approach**: The fact that volatility itself is uncertain (not constant) means there are periods where equity options are systematically mispriced relative to credit conditions. When credit markets signal stress but equity vol hasn't repriced, we're buying options at a time when volatility is about to shift regime — exactly when models are most unreliable
- **Negative skewness in stock indexes confirms put buying**: Stock indexes exhibit negative skewness — they fall faster than they rise. This is the leverage effect (more debt = more risk as equity falls). Our credit signals specifically detect increasing leverage/credit risk, so we're aligned with the natural skewness of equity markets
- **Expiration straddle concept for event-driven trades**: When a specific credit event is expected (maturity wall date, rating action date), buying short-dated ATM options just before the event exploits the same logic as expiration straddles — models undervalue the probability of a gap, and we have a fundamental reason to expect one
- **The skew function ln(E/U)/√t normalises our strike selection**: We can use this framework to compare strike selection across different names, prices, and expiration dates. A 10% OTM put on Nokia at €6.42 and a 10% OTM put on VW at €90 are at the same point on the normalised skew curve
